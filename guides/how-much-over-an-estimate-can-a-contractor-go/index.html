How much over an estimate can a contractor go?

We found no federal rule that sets a percent. The wording of your paper and state law decide. California and New Jersey require written changes on home improvement jobs.

What the paper says first

Your paper saysWhat usually follows
"Fixed price for the scope above"The price holds unless the scope changes in writing.
"Estimate, may change if…" with named casesThe price can move in those cases. Tell the customer before the cost occurs.
Only one number and no scopeBoth sides can argue. This is the paper that causes disputes.

This table is general guidance, not a legal rule. A court decides on the facts of the case.

Two state examples

  • California: a CSLB guide says that a change to the contract price must be a written change order that becomes part of the contract, signed by both sides.
  • New Jersey: N.J.A.C. 13:45A-16.2 requires a written contract for home improvement sales over $500, and all changes in its terms must also be in writing.

We did not find an official page that names a percent limit for home improvement work in general. Check your state contractor board.

How to go over without a dispute

  1. Stop before the added cost when you can.
  2. Show the customer the reason, with a photo.
  3. Write a change order with the added scope, price and days.
  4. Get a signature, then do the work.
  5. Add the change order as its own line on the next contractor invoice.

Wording that helps before the work starts

Sample wording, not legal advice: "This estimate covers the scope above. If we find hidden damage or you change the scope, we will send a written change order with the added price and days. We start that work after you sign it."

Also list the things that usually move the price on your kind of job, such as hidden pipe or rotten decking, and give a unit price for them where you can.

Short answers

Can a customer refuse to pay more than the estimate?

A customer can dispute it. Your position is stronger with a signed change order. Without one, the outcome depends on the paper, the facts and your state.

Is there a 10 percent rule?

We found no official US source for a general 10 percent rule on contractor estimates. Treat that number as a rumor until your state board confirms it.
